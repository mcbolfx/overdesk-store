7 Task Apps Worth Trying When Planning Feels Hard

August 26, 2026 · 10 min read · Productivity

Photo by Viktor Hanacek / picjumbo (free to use)

A task app is only as good as its ability to actually get used on a bad day — not the day you're feeling organized and motivated, but the day starting anything feels like wading through mud. That's the real test for anyone whose brain treats a long backlog as background noise instead of a to-do list. Seven apps below take genuinely different approaches: some lean visual, some lean flexible, and one strips things down to a single always-visible list.

This is a workflow comparison, not medical guidance, and none of the apps below are described as treating ADHD. If planning and follow-through are a persistent struggle rather than an occasional one, that's a conversation for a clinician — established care for ADHD includes options like therapy and medication that a task app isn't a substitute for.

What actually matters in a planner that works with a scattered brain

Storing tasks is the easy part — almost every app does that. The differences that matter show up around the edges:

  • Capture speed — if adding a task takes more than a few seconds, it often just doesn't get added.
  • A visible sense of time — timers, timelines, or blocks that make "how long is this actually taking" concrete instead of abstract.
  • A small daily view — somewhere that isn't the entire backlog, so today doesn't look as overwhelming as the whole list.
  • Breaking tasks down — subtasks or step-by-step splitting that turns "write the report" into something you can actually start.
  • Easy rescheduling — missed a day? Moving things forward should take one tap, not a guilt spiral.
  • More than one way to look at the same list — list, board, calendar, or timeline, since the same task list can feel completely different depending on the view.

How this list was put together

This is based on each app's own public feature and pricing pages, checked in August 2026, compared against the criteria above. It's an editorial comparison, not hands-on testing and not a clinical evaluation of any kind — nobody here is qualified to say an app treats ADHD, and none of them claim to.

The seven, one by one

Overdesk

Consider if: you want one list that's physically impossible to lose behind other windows.

Overdesk is a small checklist that floats on top of whatever else is open on your screen, so checking it doesn't mean switching away from what you're doing. It supports a couple of different modes — separate lists for, say, work and personal errands — includes a running timer with pause and reset for tracking time on a task, and reminders can be set against a specific date and time on a weekly or monthly calendar, surfacing as an on-screen alarm you have to actively dismiss rather than a notification badge that's easy to miss entirely.

The trade-off: modes separate your lists into contexts, but there's no way to filter or view across them, and no Kanban-style board or project structure the way some of the other apps here have. It's built to be one clear list per mode, plus a timer and reminders — not a full project-management system.

Tiimo

Consider if: reading a dense text list is harder for you than looking at a picture of your day.

Tiimo turns tasks into a colored, icon-based timeline rather than a list of words, with a circular countdown timer built to make elapsed time feel less abstract. Activities get an icon and color instead of being represented purely as text, and a focus mode narrows what's visible while you're mid-task.

The trade-off: the free tier is mobile-only, and the web planner plus AI-assisted planning both require the paid Pro tier (currently around $8/month or roughly $80/year). It's built around planning a day, not managing long-running projects.

Amazing Marvin

Consider if: no single fixed methodology has ever quite fit how your brain works.

Marvin ships with a large library of optional strategies you can turn on individually — task attributes for things like energy level or dread, a "spotlight" mode that narrows your view to one task, and support for several different planning philosophies rather than forcing one on you.

The trade-off: the amount of configuration available is genuinely double-edged. If tweaking a productivity system is itself one of your procrastination habits, it's worth setting a hard limit on setup time before you start.

Structured

Consider if: you want your day laid out as a timeline without learning a whole methodology first.

Structured combines tasks and calendar events into a single scrollable timeline, with an inbox for capturing things before you decide when they happen. Subtasks, recurring items, and a focus timer are available depending on platform and plan.

The trade-off: feature parity isn't consistent across platforms — the focus timer and some notification features aren't available everywhere, and recurring tasks sit behind the paid tier.

TickTick

Consider if: you'd rather have one app cover tasks, calendar, and habits than juggle three.

TickTick bundles a fairly complete set of tools — calendar and agenda views, a Pomodoro timer, habit tracking, Kanban boards, and an Eisenhower-matrix view — into a single app that works for both personal and work lists.

The trade-off: a meaningful chunk of the calendar, filtering, and statistics features are gated behind Premium, and with this many modules in one interface, it can feel busier than something purpose-built for a single use case.

Todoist

Consider if: getting a task written down in under five seconds matters more to you than anything else on this list.

Todoist's natural-language quick-add lets you type something like "call the dentist tomorrow at 3pm" and have it parsed automatically. It supports labels and custom filters, so you can build your own views — a "quick wins" filter, a "waiting on someone" filter, and so on.

The trade-off: nothing stops your Today view from growing as long as your entire backlog if you schedule too much for the same day. You have to build the guardrails yourself with filters rather than get one out of the box.

Sunsama

Consider if: you want the app to actively push back when you're planning an unrealistic day.

Sunsama centers on a daily planning ritual that compares what you're committing to against your actual calendar capacity, flagging it when a day is overbooked before it happens. It also includes an end-of-day shutdown flow for closing out and reviewing what got done.

The trade-off: it's subscription-only, requires a cloud account with no offline/local-storage option, and works best if your day is already organized around a calendar rather than a flat list.

How to actually choose

  • Want the simplest possible always-visible list? — Overdesk
  • Want your day to look like a picture, not a list? — Tiimo
  • Want to build a fully custom system? — Amazing Marvin
  • Want a simple visual timeline with no learning curve? — Structured
  • Want one app to replace three? — TickTick
  • Want the fastest possible capture? — Todoist
  • Want a guided ritual and calendar-aware planning? — Sunsama

Whatever you pick, give it a genuine trial week before judging it: can you capture a task, see today clearly, start one thing, and reschedule what didn't happen — all without quietly falling back on a second system? That's the real test, not the feature list.
